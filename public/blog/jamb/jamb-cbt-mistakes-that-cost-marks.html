7 JAMB CBT Mistakes That Quietly Cost Candidates Marks

Most lost marks come from habits, not ignorance. Fix these seven and your score moves without learning anything new.

Marks are lost in habits, not just knowledge

Two candidates can know the same amount and score very differently. The difference is often how they behave during the test: how they use time, how they read, how they handle stress. These habits can be trained, which means you can gain marks without studying a single new topic.

Here are seven mistakes that show up again and again, with a fix for each. Check the current official JAMB information for the exact exam format, because details can change, and then train around it.

Mistake 1: No time plan

With a fixed time and many questions, your average time per question is short. If the paper has 180 questions in two hours, that is about 40 seconds each. Some questions need five seconds, others two minutes. Without a plan, you will burn time on early questions and rush the rest.

Fix: Decide checkpoints. For example, by the halfway point of the time you should be about halfway through the questions. Practise with a visible timer so that the pace becomes natural.

💡
The flag habit

If a question is not clear in about 30 seconds, mark it for review, pick your best guess and move on. Come back at the end with the time left over.

Mistake 2: Leaving questions blank

Some candidates skip difficult questions and forget them. In an objective test, an unanswered question can never score, while a guess has a chance. Confirm the current marking rule, but in the standard format a guess has no penalty.

Fix: Before you submit, scan the question navigator and make sure every item has an answer. Use the last few minutes for this check, not for rereading questions you already answered well.

Mistake 3: Not eliminating options

When you are unsure, you do not have to guess among four. Remove the options you know are wrong, and your chance improves a lot. If you can remove two, you have a one in two chance.

QuestionA question gives four options and you can eliminate two of them. What is the probability that a random guess among the remaining options is correct?
  1. Start with 4 options
  2. Remove the 2 you know are wrong, leaving 2
  3. Probability = 1 correct option out of 2 remaining
Answer1/2, compared with 1/4 for a blind guess

Fix: Practise elimination aloud in your mind: this one is too extreme, that one contradicts the passage, that unit is wrong. It becomes automatic with practice.

Mistake 4: Misreading the question

Under pressure, eyes skip words. Questions such as which of the following is NOT, or find the value of x when something else is true, are easy to misread. Many wrong answers are correct solutions to a different question.

Fix: Read the last line of the question again before choosing. Mentally underline words like not, except, least and only. In calculations, check that you have answered what was asked, such as the area instead of the perimeter.

Mistake 5: Doing everything in your head

Mental calculation feels fast, but it creates slips, especially in Mathematics, Physics and Chemistry. Candidates who only use the screen lose working that they would have caught on paper.

Fix: Use the rough paper allowed at the centre, if provided, and write each step. Confirm what you may bring and use according to the official guidelines. Practise writing neat, short working at home, so that you are used to it.

Mistake 6: Screen fatigue and poor stamina

Reading for hours from a screen is tiring. Many students practise only with books, then find their concentration drops halfway through the CBT.

Fix: Build stamina. In your last weeks, do at least a few full-length timed sessions on a phone or laptop. Sleep well the night before, eat something light before you go and drink water. These simple things protect concentration.

Poor preparation habit

  • Practise only with printed books
  • Study late into the night before the exam
  • Arrive late and stressed

Better habit

  • Add timed screen-based practice
  • Sleep early and revise lightly
  • Arrive early, calm and familiar with the centre

Mistake 7: Panic after a hard question

Everyone meets a question that seems impossible. The mistake is to let it shake your confidence for the next ten questions. A wasted minute then becomes a wasted five.

Fix: Have a reset routine. Take one slow breath, flag the question and move on. Remind yourself that you do not need every mark. The exam is a competition of accumulated marks, and the next question is a fresh chance.

How to train these habits

Add one habit at a time to your normal practice. In week one, focus on timing. In week two, on elimination and the flag habit. In week three, on writing working and re-reading the question line. By week four, combine all of them in a full mock. Afterwards, review not only which answers were wrong but also what you did wrong: wasted time, a blank, a misread.

What to do today

Run a small practice session that trains behaviour, not content.

  • Do 20 questions on a screen with a visible timer
  • Flag at least two hard questions and answer them last
  • Eliminate two options on every question you are unsure about
  • Write your working on paper for every calculation
  • Note which of the seven mistakes you made most

Frequently asked questions

How can I prepare for the JAMB CBT format?

Practise on a computer, laptop or phone with a timer, and do full mixed sets. The goal is to make the screen, the navigation and the time pressure feel familiar.

Should I answer every question in the JAMB CBT?

Generally yes. Check the current official rules, but in a multiple-choice exam with no negative marking, a blank answer is a lost chance.

How do I manage time in the CBT?

Work out the average time per question, set checkpoints, and flag hard questions to return to. Do not let one question take the time of five.

What if the computer or network fails during the exam?

Stay calm and alert the centre staff immediately. Do not try to fix it yourself. Centres have procedures for technical problems.
