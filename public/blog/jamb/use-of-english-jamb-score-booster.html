Use of English in JAMB: How to Score 70+ (Comprehension, Lexis and Structure)

English is compulsory, so every candidate writes it. Here is how to stop leaking marks in comprehension, lexis and structure.

Why English deserves real study time

Everyone writes English, and most candidates assume they will do fine because they speak it daily. That is exactly why marks leak. Everyday English and exam English are different. Questions ask about precise meaning, fixed expressions and grammar rules that we break in normal talk.

The good news is that English is among the most trainable subjects. The same patterns repeat from year to year. If you practise each section deliberately, a high score is realistic. Check the current JAMB syllabus for the exact breakdown, then spend your time where the marks are.

Comprehension: read smart, not slow

Comprehension passages feel long under time pressure. The solution is a method, not speed reading.

1Read the questions first
2Skim the passage for the main idea
3Scan for keywords from each question
4Re-read that sentence carefully
5Choose and move on

Rules that save marks

  • The answer is always supported by the passage. If you need outside knowledge to choose, you are probably wrong.
  • Watch for extreme words such as always, never, only and all. Options with them are often traps.
  • For questions on the writer's attitude or purpose, look at the tone words, such as praise, complaint or warning.
  • For vocabulary-in-context questions, replace the word with each option in the sentence and see which one fits the meaning.

Lexis and structure: the biggest scoring area

This section tests vocabulary, idioms and grammar in short sentences. The questions are quick, so it is where you can collect marks fast.

Vocabulary

Synonyms and antonyms are tested in context. Do not learn a long list blindly. Learn words in sentences, and group them by theme. For example, group words for being unhappy, such as sad, gloomy and downcast.

Idioms

Idioms cannot be worked out from the words. You must know them. Read a few each day with their meanings, and use each in your own sentence.

Grammar

The most common patterns are subject-verb agreement, tenses, prepositions, and word forms. Here is a typical item.

QuestionChoose the correct option: Each of the students ____ a textbook.
  1. Find the subject: Each, which is singular even though students is nearby
  2. A singular subject needs a singular verb
  3. Options such as have are plural, so reject them
Answerhas

Notice the trap: the plural word students sits right before the blank. Examiners love placing a plural noun near a singular subject. Always find the real subject first.

Oral English: sounds and stress

Oral English questions are often the ones candidates skip. They test whether you can tell sounds apart, such as the vowel in sheep and ship, and which syllable carries the stress in a word.

You cannot learn this only from reading. Say words aloud, look up pronunciation in a dictionary and listen to clear speakers. A few minutes of this each day builds an ear that no cramming can replace. Learn the common stress patterns, like how many two-syllable nouns stress the first syllable while verbs stress the second, such as the noun record and the verb record, and then test yourself with questions.

The prescribed text, if your year includes one

Some years include a literature text as part of the English paper. Check the current JAMB syllabus and brochure to see whether one applies and which book it is. If it does, read the book itself, not only summaries. Know the plot, the main characters, the themes and a few key events. Questions often ask who said something, what it reveals or what lesson the story teaches.

A simple daily routine for English

Thirty to forty minutes a day is enough if it is varied.

  1. Day 1Two comprehension passages with a timer
  2. Day 220 lexis and structure questions plus five new idioms
  3. Day 3Oral English: sounds and stress practice
  4. Day 4Grammar rules and error log review
  5. Day 5Mixed set under time
  6. WeekendRedo every question you missed this week
💡
Keep an English error log

Every time you miss a grammar or vocabulary item, write the correct form and a short rule. Re-reading this list is a faster way to improve than any new textbook.

Common leaks and how to plug them

  • Choosing what sounds right: Familiar mistakes sound natural in Nigerian speech. Check the rule instead of trusting your ear.
  • Rushing the last questions: Do not leave the oral section to the end with two minutes left. Decide a time budget per section before you start.
  • Ignoring the small words: Prepositions such as in, on and at often decide the answer. Learn set phrases like different from and interested in.
  • Not reading all the options: The first option that looks right is sometimes only partly right.

What to do today

Start with the part where you are weakest. Your last practice set will tell you which one it is.

  • Do one comprehension passage using the read-questions-first method
  • Answer 15 lexis and structure questions and log every miss
  • Learn five idioms with meanings and write a sentence for each
  • Say ten words aloud and check their stress in a dictionary
  • Confirm from the current JAMB syllabus whether a prescribed text applies

Frequently asked questions

Why is Use of English important in JAMB?

It is compulsory for every candidate, whatever the course. A weak English score pulls your overall total down even when your other three subjects are strong.

What parts does the UTME English paper test?

It typically covers comprehension and reading, lexis and structure, oral forms and sometimes a prescribed text. Always check the current JAMB syllabus for the exact list for your year.

How do I improve my comprehension speed?

Read the questions before the passage, then scan the passage for answers. Practise two passages daily with a timer until it feels easy.

Is the lexis and structure part just grammar?

It combines vocabulary, idioms, synonyms, antonyms and grammar rules such as agreement and tenses. Learning common patterns is more useful than memorising long word lists.
