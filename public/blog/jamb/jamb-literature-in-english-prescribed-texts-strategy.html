JAMB Literature in English: How to Handle the Prescribed Texts

Literature rewards readers, not guessers. Here is how to plan your reading of the prescribed texts, what to note in each, and how to answer the question types.

What the Exam Expects

Literature in English is a subject where there is no shortcut around reading. The paper includes questions on the prescribed texts and on general literary knowledge, such as literary terms and the features of prose, drama and poetry. Candidates who have read the texts answer quickly. Candidates who have not are left guessing between options that look equally possible.

The first step is to confirm which texts are currently prescribed. The official JAMB syllabus or brochure gives the list, and it can change over time. Do not rely on a friend, an old classmate or an online summary from earlier years.

Plan Your Reading Backwards

Count the texts and estimate pages. Decide how many weeks you have, then divide. A reading plan beats good intentions.

QuestionYou have 3 prescribed texts of about 120 pages, 90 pages and 150 pages and 8 weeks before you want to start practice questions. How many pages per day do you need if you read 6 days a week?
  1. Add the pages: 120 + 90 + 150 = 360 pages
  2. Count the reading days: 8 weeks x 6 days = 48 days
  3. Divide: 360 / 48 = 7.5 pages per day
  4. Round up to 8 pages per day to leave slack
AnswerAbout 8 pages a day. That is only 15 to 20 minutes of reading, so the plan is realistic

Start with the plan, because most students who struggle with Literature simply start late. Short daily reading is more effective than one large session near the exam.

What to Note While You Read

Do not underline everything. Use a small notebook or cards with the same headings for each text:

  • Setting. Where and when, and how it affects the story.
  • Plot. Five to ten key events in order.
  • Characters. A one-line note each: who they are, what they want, how they change.
  • Themes. The big ideas, such as power, tradition versus change, love, greed or justice, with one example for each.
  • Style and language. Narrative point of view, tone, notable devices and examples.
  • Key quotes. Only a few short lines, and the speaker.
💡
One theme, one proof

For each theme, write one scene or line that proves it. Exam options often test whether you can link an idea to the right text and event.

Prose, Drama and Poetry: Different Habits

Prose (novels and stories)

Track the order of events and the relationships between characters. Pay attention to the narrator: who tells the story, and how much do they know? Knowing the point of view helps answer questions on style.

Drama

Learn the structure: acts, scenes, stage directions, soliloquy, aside, dialogue and conflict. Note what each major scene changes in the plot. Drama questions often ask about the turning point or the role of a character.

Poetry

Read each poem aloud. Write one line on what it is about, the speaker's mood and the main devices. Learn the common forms and terms, such as stanza, rhyme scheme, rhythm, refrain and imagery.

Know the Literary Devices Cold

Devices show up in extracts and in general questions, so they are some of the easiest marks. Be able to define and spot the following.

DeviceQuick meaning
SimileComparison using like or as
MetaphorDirect comparison without like or as
PersonificationGiving human qualities to non-human things
IronyMeaning that differs from what is said or expected
HyperboleDeliberate exaggeration
AlliterationRepeated starting consonant sounds
SymbolismAn object or action that stands for an idea
QuestionIdentify the device: "The wind whispered through the empty compound."
  1. Look for a non-human thing doing a human action: the wind whispers
  2. Check for like or as: none appears, so it is not a simile
  3. Check whether it is a direct comparison: the wind is not said to be something else
AnswerPersonification, because the wind is given the human action of whispering

How to Answer the Question Types

Most questions fall into a few patterns. Practise each one.

  • Who said or did what? This tests your character notes. Link names to key scenes.
  • What is the theme? Choose the option that matches the main idea of the passage, not just a detail in it.
  • What device is used? Match the extract to the definition.
  • What does the extract reveal? Look for tone, character or setting clues.
  • General terms. Definitions of genres and features, such as tragedy, comedy, satire, elegy and ode.

Eliminate options that contradict the text. When two options look close, pick the one supported by a specific event or line you remember.

Revision and Mistakes to Avoid

  • Relying only on summaries and film adaptations. They often drop details or change the plot.
  • Reading late and fast, so that characters blur together.
  • Learning devices without practising them on extracts.
  • Ignoring poetry because it feels hard. Poems are short, so you can revise them quickly.
  • Skipping past questions. They show you how the examiner phrases things.

What to Do Today

  • Confirm the current prescribed texts on the official JAMB syllabus
  • Count the pages and divide by your available days
  • Set a daily reading slot
  • Start a notebook with headings for setting and plot and characters and themes
  • Learn the seven literary devices in the table
  • Try five past questions on devices

Frequently asked questions

How do I find the current JAMB Literature texts?

Check the official JAMB syllabus or brochure for the current list of prescribed texts. The list can change, so confirm before you buy or read anything.

Do I need to read the full books or can I use summaries?

Read the books. Summaries help you revise, but questions often test details, characters and language that summaries leave out.

How do I remember so many characters?

Keep a one-line card per character: who they are, what they want and one key event. Review the cards after each chapter.

What literary devices should I know?

Know the common ones such as simile, metaphor, personification, irony, symbolism, hyperbole, alliteration and imagery, and practise spotting them in short extracts.

How long does it take to prepare for Literature?

It depends on the number and length of the texts and how fast you read. Make a reading schedule, finish the books first, then spend the remaining time on practice questions.
