How to Use JAMB Past Questions Properly (Most Students Do It Wrong)

Past questions are gold, but only if you use them as a diagnostic tool and not as a reading material to cram.

The common mistake

Many candidates treat a past-questions book like a novel. They read the question, read the answer, nod, and move on. It feels productive. It is not. You are practising recognition, not recall, and the exam asks for recall.

Think of a past question as a doctor's test result. Its value is in what it tells you about your weak points. A wrong answer is not a failure. It is the most useful information in your whole preparation, provided you do something with it.

What past questions can and cannot do for you

They show you the style of questions, the topics that are asked often and the level of difficulty. They train your speed. They also expose gaps you did not know you had.

What they cannot do is replace learning. If you do not understand the chemistry of acids and bases, a hundred acid-base questions will only teach you that you are confused a hundred times. Use the questions to find the problem, then go back to the textbook and fix it.

⚠️
Do not cram answers

Exam questions are reworded and the options are shuffled. If you only remember that the answer was B, you will lose the mark when the question changes.

The five-step method

  1. Pick a topicStudy the topic from your notes or textbook first
  2. Attempt without helpAnswer 15 to 20 questions on it with no book open and a timer running
  3. Mark honestlyTick right answers and mark every wrong or guessed answer
  4. Diagnose each missWrite why you lost it: did not know, misread, calculation slip or ran out of time
  5. Fix and retestStudy the gap, then redo the same missed questions a few days later

The fourth step is where the learning happens. Most students skip it. Keep a small notebook called an error log with three columns: the topic, what went wrong and the correct idea in your own words. Review it every weekend.

A worked example: how to learn from one question

Here is how to treat a single wrong answer, using a Mathematics question.

QuestionSolve for x: 2x+1 = 32.
  1. Write 32 as a power of 2: 32 = 25
  2. Both sides now have the same base, so the powers must be equal: x + 1 = 5
  3. Subtract 1 from both sides
Answerx = 4

Say you got this wrong. In your error log you would not just write "x = 4". You would write: "Indices: when bases match, equate the powers. Rewrite numbers as powers of the same base first." Then you would find three more questions of this type and solve them the same day. One missed question becomes a skill.

Timing and the CBT environment

The UTME is written on a computer, and the time per question is short. As a rough guide, if you have two hours for 180 questions, you have about 40 seconds on average per question. Check the current official JAMB information for the exact format, because it can change.

That is why timed practice matters. Once you are comfortable, set a timer and practise in blocks. Use a phone or laptop where you can, so that reading from a screen and clicking an option feel natural. Learn these habits:

  • Answer the questions you know first and flag the difficult ones.
  • Never leave a question blank, because a guess costs nothing when there is no negative marking. Confirm the current rule with JAMB.
  • For calculations, do the working on paper, and do not trust mental arithmetic under pressure.

Mixing it up as exam day approaches

Early on, practise by topic. In the final month, switch to full mixed sets, because the exam does not announce the topic before each question. Interleaving topics is harder, but it trains you to identify what a question is testing, which is half the battle.

How most students practise

  • Read answers straight away
  • Do the same set again and again
  • Ignore wrong answers
  • Practise untimed all the time

How to practise

  • Attempt first and check after
  • Redo only the questions you missed
  • Log every error and its cause
  • Add a timer from the middle of your preparation

What to do today

Pick one topic you studied this week and run the five steps on it. Keep it small and finish it.

  • Choose one topic you have already studied
  • Answer 15 questions on it with a timer and no notes
  • Mark them and circle every miss
  • Write the cause of each miss in an error log
  • Schedule a retest of the missed questions in three days

Frequently asked questions

Should I memorise JAMB past question answers?

No. Questions are rarely repeated word for word, but the same ideas and question styles come back. Memorised answers fail when the wording changes. Understand why the answer is right.

How many years of past questions should I practise?

Use as many years as you can reach, but quality matters more than quantity. One set that you mark carefully and learn from is worth more than five that you only skim.

When should I start doing past questions?

As soon as you have studied a topic. Do not wait until you finish the syllabus. Early use shows you how the topic is tested.

Are answers in past question books always correct?

Not always. Some printed answers contain errors. If an answer looks wrong after you have worked it out carefully, check your textbook or ask a teacher before accepting it.
