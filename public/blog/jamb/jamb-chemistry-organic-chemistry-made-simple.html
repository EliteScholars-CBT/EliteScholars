Organic Chemistry Made Simple for JAMB Candidates

Organic chemistry looks scary because of the long names. Learn the families and naming rules and it becomes easy.

Michael Joseph
·📅 12 November 2026·⏱ 7 min read

Organic chemistry puts many JAMB candidates off because of long names such as 2-methylbutane or but-1-ene. But the subject is far more orderly than it looks. Almost everything depends on three ideas: the families of compounds (homologous series), their general formulas and a few naming rules. Learn those, practise daily, and organic chemistry becomes some of the most predictable marks in the paper.

Start with the families

Organic compounds are built around carbon. They are grouped into families, each with a functional group that decides how it behaves. Learn this table first.

FamilyGeneral formulaFunctional groupExample
AlkanesCnH2n+2Single C-C bondsEthane, C2H6
AlkenesCnH2nC=C double bondEthene, C2H4
AlkynesCnH2n-2C≡C triple bondEthyne, C2H2
AlkanolsCnH2n+1OH-OHEthanol, C2H5OH
Alkanoic acidsCnH2n+1COOH-COOHEthanoic acid, CH3COOH
EstersRCOOR'-COO-Ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOC2H5

Alkanes are saturated (only single bonds). Alkenes and alkynes are unsaturated. Use the general formula to check any molecule quickly.

Alkane checkCnH2n+2With 6 carbons, hydrogens = 2(6) + 2 = 14, so the formula is C6H14

Learn the prefixes

The number of carbons in the longest chain gives the stem of the name.

1 carbonmeth
2 carbonseth
3 carbonsprop
4 carbonsbut
5 carbonspent
6 carbonshex

Seven to ten carbons are hept, oct, non and dec. The ending then tells the family: -ane for alkanes, -ene for alkenes, -yne for alkynes, -ol for alkanols and -oic acid for alkanoic acids.

Naming in four steps

Follow the same steps every time and you will rarely go wrong.

  1. Find the longest chainCount the carbons in the longest continuous chain, including through the functional group
  2. Name the stem and endingUse the prefix and the family ending, for example but plus ane
  3. Number the chainStart from the end that gives the lowest number to the functional group or double bond, then to branches
  4. Add branchesWrite each branch as methyl, ethyl and so on, with its number, before the stem
QuestionName the compound CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3
  1. Find the longest chain: four carbons in a row, so the stem is but
  2. Check bonds: all single, so the ending is ane
  3. Number from either end: the branch sits on carbon 2
  4. Name the branch: one carbon is methyl
Answer2-methylbutane. Its formula is C5H12, which fits CnH2n+2 with n = 5.

Isomers

Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures. For C4H10, there are two: butane (a straight chain of four carbons) and 2-methylpropane (a chain of three with a methyl branch). When a question asks how many isomers a formula has, draw chains systematically: longest chain first, then shorter chains with branches.

The reactions you must know

Group them by family and write each in a table with conditions. Check the current JAMB syllabus for the exact list.

ReactionReactants and conditionsProducts
Complete combustion of alkanesAlkane plus excess oxygenCarbon dioxide and water
Substitution (alkanes)Methane plus chlorine, ultraviolet lightChloromethane and hydrogen chloride
Addition (alkenes)Ethene plus bromine1,2-dibromoethane
FermentationGlucose with yeastEthanol and carbon dioxide
EsterificationAlkanoic acid plus alkanol, concentrated sulphuric acid, warmEster and water

Balance the equations. For ethane: 2C2H6 + 7O2 gives 4CO2 + 6H2O. Count each atom: carbon is 4 on both sides, hydrogen is 12 on both sides and oxygen is 14 on both sides.

For the alkene test, shake with bromine water. An alkene turns it from brown or orange to colourless, because bromine adds across the double bond. Alkanes do not do this at room temperature.

💡
Substitution versus addition

Alkanes are saturated, so they substitute (one atom replaces another). Alkenes are unsaturated, so they add (the double bond opens). Ask yourself which one the molecule can do.

Common mistakes

Common mistake

  • Counting only the chain written in a straight line
  • Numbering from the wrong end
  • Mixing up alkene and alkyne formulas
  • Memorising reactions without conditions

Better way

  • Look for the longest chain even when it bends
  • Give the functional group the lowest number
  • Check CnH2n against CnH2n-2
  • Write reactants, conditions and products together

A daily practice plan

Do ten naming questions a day for a week. Day one: alkanes. Day two: alkenes and alkynes. Day three: alkanols and acids. Day four: isomers. Day five to six: reactions and tests. Day seven: a mixed set of past JAMB questions. Mark them and log each error. Use the official syllabus as your checklist.

What to do today

  • Copy the family table onto one page and learn it
  • Say the prefixes meth to dec aloud five times
  • Name five alkanes and alkenes using the four steps
  • Write the ethane combustion equation and balance it
  • Do ten past JAMB organic questions and log your errors

Frequently asked questions

What is a homologous series?

It is a family of organic compounds with the same functional group and a similar general formula. Each member differs from the next by a CH2 unit.

How do I remember the carbon prefixes?

Learn them in order: meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6), hept (7), oct (8), non (9), dec (10). Say them aloud daily until they come without thinking.

How do I test for an alkene?

Alkenes decolourise bromine water, and also acidified potassium permanganate. Alkanes do not react in the same way at room temperature.

Do I need to learn every reaction?

Focus on the ones in the current JAMB Chemistry syllabus: combustion, substitution, addition, esterification and fermentation, with their conditions. Check the official syllabus for the full list.

Why do I keep misnaming compounds?

Usually the longest chain was not counted correctly, or numbering started from the wrong end. Follow the same steps every time.
