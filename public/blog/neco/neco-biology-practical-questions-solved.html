NECO Biology practical questions: specimens, spotting and drawing, solved with examples

How to handle spotting, drawing, food tests, magnification and ecology questions in the NECO Biology practical, with worked numbers and the marking habits that earn credit.

What practical Biology really tests

The Biology practical is less about memorising and more about looking carefully and writing exactly what you see. Typical tasks include identifying specimens, drawing and labelling, doing food tests, measuring and calculating, and answering questions that link what you observe to a principle you learned in class. Whether you sit the real practical or the alternative-to-practical paper, the skills are the same. Check the current NECO syllabus for the format.

Spotting: how to identify specimens fast

Spotting questions show you a specimen, a model or a picture and ask for the name, a feature and often a function or adaptation. Use a quick routine for each one.

1Look at the whole specimen
2Note the visible features
3Name it
4State the feature asked
5Link feature to function

Do not just write "leaf". Be precise: "a dicotyledonous leaf with net venation". Here are common specimens and what examiners usually ask.

  • Fish (tilapia): streamlined body (reduces water resistance), gills protected by the operculum, lateral line for sensing vibrations, fins for balance and movement.
  • Grasshopper: three body parts, six legs, wings, spiracles for breathing, chewing mouthparts.
  • Bean and maize seeds: bean is dicot with two cotyledons, maize is monocot with one cotyledon and a single seed leaf, with the endosperm present.
  • Hibiscus flower: sepals, petals, stamens (anther and filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary).
  • Bones, teeth and skulls: identify the type by shape. Incisors are chisel-shaped for cutting, canines pointed for tearing, molars broad for grinding.

When the question says "state one adaptive feature", always add the reason, for example "sharp canines for tearing flesh". The reason is where the second mark sits.

Drawing and labelling: the rules that earn marks

Drawing is one of the most predictable mark sources, because the rules are the same every year. Candidates lose marks by breaking the basics, not by lacking talent.

Marks lost

  • Pen or coloured pencil
  • Shading and sketchy lines
  • Tiny drawing in a corner
  • Label lines that cross or have arrowheads
  • Labels written on the drawing

Marks earned

  • Sharp HB pencil
  • Single, clear, continuous lines
  • Drawing that fills about half the space or more
  • Straight, ruled label lines that touch the part
  • Labels written in a neat column away from the drawing
  • Draw what you see, not what you remember from the textbook.
  • Give the drawing a title (for example: Drawing of the lateral view of the specimen).
  • Where asked, show the magnification and a direction or view (top, side, cut surface).
  • Keep the proportions right: if one part is twice the length of another in the specimen, it should be about the same ratio on paper.

Magnification: a calculation you must get right

Magnification = size of drawing / actual size of specimen. Both lengths must be in the same unit.

QuestionA student measures a leaf as 30 mm long. Her drawing of the leaf is 90 mm long. Calculate the magnification.
  1. Write the formula: magnification = drawing size / actual size
  2. Check the units: both are in millimetres
  3. Substitute: 90 / 30
  4. Divide: 3
AnswerMagnification = x3, meaning the drawing is three times as large as the leaf

If the drawing is smaller than the specimen, the magnification is less than 1, for example x0.5. A common mistake is dividing the other way round, so ask yourself: is my drawing bigger or smaller, and does my answer agree?

Food tests: record observations, not guesses

The food tests appear often. Write the reagent, the method, the observation and the conclusion. Examiners want the colour you actually saw, and the conclusion that follows.

Food classReagentPositive result
StarchIodine solutionBlue-black colour
Reducing sugarBenedict's solution, then heatBlue turns green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate
ProteinBiuret test (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper sulphate)Purple or violet colour
Fat or oilEthanol emulsion test (shake with ethanol, then add water)Cloudy white emulsion
Vitamin CDCPIP solutionBlue DCPIP is decolourised

Write results like this: "Observation: the mixture turned brick-red on heating. Conclusion: reducing sugar is present." If nothing changes, say "the solution remained blue, so reducing sugar is absent". Heating Benedict's solution in a water bath, not on a direct flame, is a safety point examiners like to see.

Ecology calculations: quadrat and capture-recapture

Population estimates are practical favourites because they test method as well as numbers.

QuestionUsing a 1 square metre quadrat, a student finds an average of 4 plants per quadrat in a field of area 200 square metres. Estimate the total number of plants.
  1. Average number per square metre = 4
  2. Total area = 200 square metres
  3. Multiply: 4 x 200
AnswerAbout 800 plants
QuestionIn a capture-recapture study, 20 tilapia are caught, marked and released. Later 30 are caught and 6 of them carry marks. Estimate the population.
  1. Population = (number marked first x number caught second) / number of marked recaptured
  2. Substitute: (20 x 30) / 6
  3. 600 / 6 = 100
AnswerEstimated population = 100 fish

Add the assumptions when asked: marked animals mix freely, marks do not harm the animal or fall off, and there is no large birth, death or migration between samples. Random placement of quadrats (for example by throwing it over your shoulder) avoids bias.

Writing the answers: the language of practical Biology

  • Use the past tense for what you did and observed.
  • Describe observations (what you see) separately from deductions (what it means).
  • Use correct names: stomata, operculum, cotyledon, anther. Avoid "the thing at the top".
  • Give the unit with every measurement and keep to the same number of decimal places.
  • When a table is requested, rule it, give each column a heading with units and fill every cell.
🔑
Sources of error

If asked how to improve an experiment, say repeat and take the average, use a more accurate measuring instrument, keep other variables constant and control the temperature.

What to do today

  • Draw one leaf and one flower with a title and neat labels
  • Measure a specimen and calculate the magnification
  • Learn the five food tests and their colour results
  • Practise one quadrat and one capture-recapture calculation
  • List three adaptive features of a fish and a grasshopper with their functions

Frequently asked questions

What is the NECO Biology practical about?

It tests observation and skills: identifying and describing specimens, drawing and labelling, carrying out tests such as food tests, taking measurements and answering questions on ecology, feeding and adaptation. Check the current syllabus for the exact format.

How do I draw biological specimens for marks?

Use a sharp pencil, a clear single outline, no shading, a large drawing that fills most of the space, straight label lines that do not cross, and a title with the magnification where asked.

What if I take the alternative to practical paper?

The alternative paper uses diagrams, photos and described experiments instead of real specimens, so practise reading diagrams, naming parts and explaining results. The same skills of observation and accurate language apply.

How do I calculate magnification?

Magnification equals the size of the drawing divided by the actual size of the specimen. Measure both in the same unit, usually millimetres.
