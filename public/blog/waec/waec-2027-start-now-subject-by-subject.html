WAEC 2027: A Subject-by-Subject Study Plan for SS3 Students

Ten months sounds long until your mock exam arrives. Here is how to divide your year across your nine subjects.

Why plan by subject and not by day

A year looks long. SS3 disappears fast, with school tests, mock exams, holidays and the stress of everything else. Students who plan only by the day soon find that some subjects got attention and others quietly got none.

A subject-by-subject plan fixes this. You decide how much of the year each subject deserves, based on its importance and your current strength, and then you break that down into terms and weeks. Check the current WAEC timetable and syllabus on the official portal to keep your plan accurate, and tie your plan to your school calendar.

Step 1: sort your subjects into three tiers

List all your subjects and rate each one honestly.

  • Tier 1, protect and fix: English Language and Mathematics, plus any subject required for your course where you are weak. These get the most time.
  • Tier 2, build steadily: subjects where you are average. They need regular practice to become strong.
  • Tier 3, maintain: subjects where you are already strong. Keep them warm with short regular sessions.

This is important because a credit pass in English and Mathematics is usually needed for admission, so these two should never be left to the end. Always check the specific requirement for your intended course and school.

Step 2: split the year into three phases

  1. First termCover the syllabus topics you have not met, start Tier 1 repair, and begin past questions topic by topic
  2. Second termFinish the syllabus, do mixed past-question sets, and take the school mock seriously
  3. Final stretchFull papers under timed conditions, correct the weak points, and light revision of notes

The key idea is that learning comes first and testing comes throughout. Do not wait until the final months to see what is on the papers.

Step 3: understand what each paper rewards

Most WAEC subjects have an objective paper and a theory or essay paper. They reward different skills.

Objective papers

These test breadth and accuracy. Practise with timed multiple-choice sets, and learn from your wrong answers.

Theory and essay papers

These test depth and presentation. Marks come from showing steps, using correct terms and answering exactly what is asked. In Mathematics, working earns marks even when the final answer is wrong. In sciences, labelled diagrams and correct units matter. In English, structure, correct grammar and clear paragraphs make the difference.

🔑
Answer the command word

Words like state, explain, describe and compare ask for different things. State needs a short fact. Explain needs a reason. Compare needs both sides. Using the wrong approach loses marks even with the right knowledge.

Step 4: a worked example of a weekly plan

Suppose a student has English, Mathematics, Biology, Chemistry, Physics, Economics, Government, Literature and Civic Education. Here is one way to spread a school week, with weekends for deeper work.

QuestionA student has about 12 free study hours per week. How could these hours be split sensibly across Tier 1, Tier 2 and Tier 3 subjects, when English and Mathematics are Tier 1, Biology and Chemistry are Tier 2, and the rest are Tier 3?
  1. Give Tier 1 roughly half the time: 6 hours, so 3 hours each for English and Mathematics
  2. Give Tier 2 about a third: 4 hours, so 2 hours each for Biology and Chemistry
  3. Give Tier 3 the remaining 2 hours, rotating the other subjects in short sessions
  4. Add up: 6 + 4 + 2 = 12 hours
AnswerEnglish 3, Mathematics 3, Biology 2, Chemistry 2, remaining subjects share 2 hours

The split is only an example. Adjust it after each test. If your Physics result is poor, move an hour from a strong subject to it for the next month.

Step 5: use past questions and the syllabus as your guide

For every subject, get the current official syllabus and tick topics as you finish them. Then use past questions to see how those topics are examined. For theory subjects, practise writing full answers and compare them with a mark scheme or a teacher's feedback.

Make a short revision sheet for each subject: formulas, definitions, diagrams and key facts. Short sheets are far easier to review in the last weeks than a whole notebook.

Protecting your energy in SS3

  • Study at fixed times so it becomes routine and not a daily argument with yourself.
  • Sleep properly. A tired mind retains little.
  • Join or form a small study group for one session a week, but keep it focused.
  • Take the school mock as a rehearsal, then fix every weakness it shows.
  • Stay away from malpractice schemes. They put your result and future at risk, and they cannot replace real preparation.

What to do today

Planning takes less than an hour and can shape the rest of the year.

  • List all your WAEC subjects and sort them into three tiers
  • Write the three phases of your year on one page
  • Download the current syllabus for your two weakest subjects
  • Decide your weekly hours and write them in a timetable
  • Do ten past questions in your weakest subject tonight

Frequently asked questions

How many subjects should I prepare for in WAEC?

Most candidates register for a set of subjects that includes English and Mathematics, as required by their school and their intended course. Check the current WAEC rules and your school's registration list.

Which subjects need the most time?

Those where you are weakest and those your course needs. Mathematics and English matter for almost every admission, so protect time for them.

When should I start past questions for WAEC?

From the first term. Practise each topic with past questions soon after studying it, and move to full papers closer to the exam.

Can I study WAEC and JAMB together?

Yes. Many topics overlap. Study the topic once in depth, then practise WAEC-style theory and JAMB-style objective questions on it.
