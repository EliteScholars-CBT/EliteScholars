Concord (subject-verb agreement): the rules that decide most exam questions

Concord means the verb must agree with its subject. Learn the handful of rules that decide most WAEC and JAMB lexis and structure questions, with worked examples.

What concord means and why it is marked so often

Concord simply means agreement. In the exam, the type that matters most is subject-verb agreement: the verb must match its subject in number. A singular subject needs a singular verb ("The girl sings"). A plural subject needs a plural verb ("The girls sing").

That sounds easy, and it is, until the examiner puts a long phrase between the subject and the verb, or joins two subjects with or. Most wrong answers come from reading the wrong word as the subject. So the first habit to build is this: find the real subject first, then choose the verb.

🔑
The golden habit

Cross out every phrase that sits between the subject and the verb, such as "of the students" or "together with his friends". Then read the sentence again. The verb must agree with what is left.

Rule 1: Ignore the extra phrases

Words like together with, along with, as well as, in addition to and with do not add to the subject. They are only extras.

  • The principal, together with the teachers, is in the hall.
  • The box of oranges was heavy. (The subject is "box", not "oranges".)
  • One of my brothers lives in Abuja. (The subject is "one".)

Rule 2: And, or, nor

Two subjects joined by and are plural: "Tunde and Ada are cousins." The exception is when the two words name one idea or one person: "Bread and butter is my breakfast." "The chairman and secretary has resigned" (one person holds both jobs).

With or, nor, either ... or, neither ... nor, use the proximity rule: the verb agrees with the subject closest to it.

Wrong

  • Neither the boys nor the girl are ready
  • Either the teacher or the students is coming

Correct

  • Neither the boys nor the girl is ready
  • Either the teacher or the students are coming

Rule 3: Words that are always singular

These take a singular verb: each, every, everyone, everybody, anyone, no one, nobody, someone, either, neither, and expressions like one of, each of, many a.

  • Each of the candidates has a pencil.
  • Everybody was present.
  • Neither of the two answers is correct.
  • Many a student has failed this question.

Uncountable nouns are also singular: information, advice, furniture, luggage, equipment, news, evidence. So "The information is useful", never "informations are". The same applies to subjects that end in -s but are singular in meaning: mathematics, physics, economics, politics, news. Amounts of money, time and distance treated as one quantity are singular too: "Ten thousand naira is a lot of money."

Rule 4: Collective nouns and "the number"

A collective noun (team, committee, class, family, jury) takes a singular verb when the group acts as one unit, and a plural verb when the members act separately.

  • The committee has reached its decision. (one unit)
  • The committee are arguing among themselves. (members acting separately)

Also note: "The number of students is rising" (singular) but "A number of students are absent" (plural). Plural nouns like police, people, cattle take plural verbs: "The police are here."

Worked examples in exam style

QuestionChoose the correct option: The list of candidates ___ pinned to the notice board. (A) are (B) were (C) is (D) have been
  1. Find the real subject: "The list of candidates" ... the subject is "list"
  2. "of candidates" is an extra phrase, so cross it out
  3. "list" is singular, so the verb must be singular
  4. Only "is" is singular and present
AnswerThe correct option is (C) is.
QuestionChoose the correct option: Neither the driver nor the passengers ___ hurt in the accident. (A) was (B) were (C) is (D) has been
  1. This is a neither ... nor sentence, so use the proximity rule
  2. The subject nearest to the verb is "passengers"
  3. "Passengers" is plural, so the verb is plural
  4. The past tense fits the accident
AnswerThe correct option is (B) were.
QuestionChoose the correct option: Mathematics ___ my favourite subject. (A) are (B) were being (C) have been (D) is
  1. "Mathematics" ends in -s but names one subject
  2. It is singular, so the verb must be singular
AnswerThe correct option is (D) is.

Common traps to watch for

  • Inverted sentences: "There are five books on the table." The subject "books" comes after the verb.
  • Relative clauses: "She is one of the girls who are chosen." The verb "are" agrees with "girls".
  • Pronouns: a verb must agree with I, you, he, they too: "I am", "he has", "they have".
  • Tense: keep the same tense within a sentence unless the meaning changes.

In your own essays, concord mistakes cost marks quickly because the examiner sees them on every page. Read your last paragraph aloud in your head before you submit. Check the syllabus and past questions for the exact style your exam body uses.

What to do today

  • Write the four rules in your own words
  • Take ten past questions and underline the real subject before choosing
  • Make five sentences using neither ... nor
  • List ten uncountable nouns and use each in a sentence
  • Read one page of your last essay and hunt for concord errors

Frequently asked questions

What is concord in English?

Concord is agreement in grammar. The most tested type is subject-verb agreement: a singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb.

Is 'news' singular or plural?

News is uncountable and takes a singular verb, so we say 'The news is good'. The same is true of information, advice, furniture and equipment.

Does 'together with' make a subject plural?

No. Phrases such as together with, along with, as well as and in addition to are extras. The verb agrees with the main subject before them.

How do I handle 'either ... or' and 'neither ... nor'?

Use the proximity rule: the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it. 'Neither the boys nor the girl is ready.'

Where does concord appear in the exams?

In the lexis and structure section of WAEC and JAMB English, in fill-in-the-gap and choose-the-correct-option questions, and in the marking of your essays.
