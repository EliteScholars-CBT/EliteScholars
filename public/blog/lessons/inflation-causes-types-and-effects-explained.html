Inflation: Causes, Types, Effects and How the CBN Responds

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. This lesson covers its types, causes, who wins and loses, the formula for the inflation rate, and the tools the CBN uses to control it.

What inflation really means

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level over time. Two words matter: sustained and general. If only the price of tomatoes rises for one week, that is not inflation. If most prices keep rising month after month, that is.

The result is that the value of money falls. N1,000 buys fewer items than it did last year. In Nigeria, the National Bureau of Statistics measures inflation using the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks the cost of a typical basket of goods and services.

Inflation rateInflation rate = (new index - old index) / old index x 100Use the CPI or price of the same basket in two periods
QuestionA basket of goods cost N50,000 last year and costs N56,000 this year. Find the inflation rate. What is N50,000 salary worth now if prices rise 20 percent?
  1. Change in price = 56,000 - 50,000 = 6,000
  2. Rate = 6,000 / 50,000 x 100 = 12 percent
  3. With 20 percent inflation, real value = 50,000 / 1.20 = 41,666.67
AnswerInflation is 12 percent. The N50,000 salary buys only what about N41,667 bought before.

Types of inflation

  • Demand-pull inflation: too much money chasing too few goods. Total demand rises faster than output.
  • Cost-push inflation: production costs rise (wages, fuel, electricity, imported inputs) and firms raise prices.
  • Imported inflation: rising prices of imports, often when the naira weakens against foreign currencies.
  • Structural inflation: caused by weaknesses in the economy, such as poor roads and storage that make food scarce and expensive.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid price rises that destroy confidence in money.
  • Creeping inflation: slow, mild price rises over a long time.

Common confusion

  • Demand-pull and cost-push are the same thing
  • Inflation means one price rose

Clear distinction

  • Demand-pull starts with spending, cost-push starts with costs
  • Inflation is a general, sustained rise

Causes in the Nigerian context

Examiners like examples from home. Valid causes include:

  • Excess money supply: when more money circulates than goods available.
  • Heavy reliance on imports: a weaker naira raises the naira cost of imported goods and inputs.
  • Transport and energy costs: higher fuel prices raise the cost of moving food and goods.
  • Poor infrastructure: bad roads and weak storage cause food losses and push prices up.
  • Insecurity or disruptions to farming: lower output of food.
  • Government deficit spending funded by creating money.

Effects of inflation

Losers: people on fixed incomes such as pensioners, savers when interest is below inflation, lenders on fixed-rate loans, and workers whose pay is not adjusted. Poor families spend most income on food, so they feel it first.

Possible winners: borrowers repaying fixed loans with money that has lost value, and people who own real assets such as land whose prices rise.

Wider effects: uncertainty reduces investment, exports can become less competitive, there is a risk of workers demanding higher wages (which can fuel cost-push inflation), and the cost of living rises. Menu costs and shoe-leather costs are also named in some syllabuses: firms must keep changing price lists and people waste time hunting for bargains.

How the CBN responds

The Central Bank of Nigeria controls inflation mainly through monetary policy, meaning management of money supply and interest rates. Its main tools:

  1. Monetary policy rate (MPR): raising it makes borrowing from the CBN and then from banks more expensive, so spending falls.
  2. Cash reserve ratio (CRR): raising it forces banks to keep more cash with the CBN, so they lend less.
  3. Open market operations: selling government securities takes money out of the economy.
  4. Liquidity ratio: raising it makes banks hold more liquid assets and lend less.
  5. Moral suasion: persuading banks to restrict credit.

The government can also help through fiscal policy: reducing its spending, raising taxes, and investing in roads, power and agriculture to expand supply. Tight monetary policy has a trade-off, because high interest rates can slow business growth and job creation. For an essay answer, mention that the CBN must balance price stability against growth.

Frequently asked questions

What is inflation in simple terms?

It is a continuous rise in the general level of prices, which means each naira buys less than before.

What is the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation?

Demand-pull happens when total spending is more than the economy can supply. Cost-push happens when production costs rise, for example higher fuel or wage costs, and firms pass these on in higher prices.

How does the CBN control inflation?

Mainly through monetary policy: raising the monetary policy rate, raising the cash reserve ratio, and selling securities in open market operations, all of which reduce the money available for spending.

Who gains and who loses from inflation?

Borrowers with fixed-rate loans can gain, while people on fixed incomes and savers with low interest rates lose purchasing power.
