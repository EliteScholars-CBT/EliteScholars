How to Turn Your Lecture Notes Into a Study Pack in 30 Minutes

Notes are only useful if you can test yourself with them. Here is a 30-minute method that converts a lecture's notes into questions, flashcards and a one-page summary.

The Problem With Notes

You attend the lecture, write pages of notes, and then close the file until exam week. When you open it again, everything looks familiar, but you cannot remember it. The notes became a record, not a tool.

The fix is simple: turn notes into things you can test yourself on. This takes about 30 minutes per lecture. Do it within a day or two of the class and you will already have done a lot of revision.

The 30-Minute Method at a Glance

1Mark key points 10 min
2Write questions 15 min
3Build a one-page summary 5 min
4Test yourself next day

Here is each step in detail.

Step 1: Mark the Key Points (10 Minutes)

Read your notes once with a pen, not a highlighter. Your job is to mark five types of content:

  • Definitions. Terms the lecturer defined.
  • Lists and steps. For example, "four causes of inflation" or "stages of the process".
  • Formulas and rules. With what each symbol means.
  • Comparisons. Differences between two ideas, such as simple versus compound interest.
  • Examples the lecturer stressed. If it was repeated or written on the board, it matters.

Put a question mark next to anything unclear. Those are the points to ask about, or check in the handout or textbook.

Step 2: Write Questions From the Notes (15 Minutes)

For each marked point, write a question. Use different question types so you are not only recalling definitions:

  • What is...? for definitions.
  • List the... for steps and categories.
  • Differentiate between... and... for comparisons.
  • Why...? and How...? for processes and reasons.
  • Calculate... for formulas, with a number you made up.
QuestionConvert this note into three questions: "Demand is the quantity of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price. The law of demand says that, other things equal, quantity demanded falls when price rises."
  1. Pull out the definition: what is demand?
  2. Pull out the rule: what does the law of demand state?
  3. Create an application question: if the price of garri rises and other things stay equal, what happens to quantity demanded?
AnswerQ1: Define demand. Q2: State the law of demand. Q3: What happens to quantity demanded when the price of garri rises, other things equal? The answer to Q3 is that it falls

Notice how one short note produced three questions that test definition, rule and application. Do this across your whole lecture and you will have 8 to 15 questions.

Write them on flashcards, or on one side of a page with the answers hidden on the other. If you prefer to use an app, you can upload notes to a study tool such as EliteScholars and generate questions from them, then edit what it produces so that it matches your lecturer's wording.

Step 3: Build a One-Page Summary (5 Minutes)

Create one page with the lecture's skeleton: the title, three to five main headings, the key definitions in short form, and any formulas. This is your quick-review sheet.

A good summary page has:

  • A heading for each main idea.
  • Short phrases, not sentences.
  • A small box for formulas.
  • A list of the questions you got wrong most often. This will grow as you test yourself.
💡
Make the summary last

Write the summary after the questions. Choosing what to leave out forces you to understand the structure of the lecture.

Step 4: Test Yourself the Next Day

The pack is only useful when you use it. The next day, answer your questions without looking. Put an X next to any you miss. Re-test the missed ones two days later, and again a week later.

  1. Same dayMake the pack
  2. Next dayTest all questions
  3. 3 days laterTest missed questions only
  4. 1 week laterTest again and add new lectures
  5. Before examsMix all lecture packs together for full-course practice

By exam week you will have tested yourself on each topic several times, which is far more effective than reading everything once more.

Handling Messy Notes and Missed Lectures

  • Messy notes: Rewrite only the confusing parts, not the whole page. Then write questions from those parts.
  • Missed lectures: Borrow notes from a serious coursemate, then do the same method. Mark anything you cannot follow and ask.
  • Slides and handouts: Do not treat them as notes. They are material for questions, so mark the key slides and write questions from them.
  • Calculation courses: Make question cards with a problem on the front and the worked solution on the back. Try to solve the problem from scratch before flipping.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Making questions so long that they cannot be answered quickly.
  • Copying the note text word for word onto the card. Put the answer in your own short words.
  • Doing it all in one night before the exam. The method works best when spread over weeks.
  • Ignoring what your lecturer emphasised. Check past questions to see how that lecturer asks.

What to Do Today

  • Pick one lecture's notes from this week
  • Mark definitions and lists with a pen
  • Write at least eight questions from them
  • Create a one-page summary
  • Put a reminder to test yourself tomorrow

Frequently asked questions

How do I make flashcards from lecture notes?

Pick one fact, definition or step per card. Write a question on the front and a short answer on the back. Keep each card small.

How long should it take to turn notes into a study pack?

About 30 minutes per lecture or topic is a good target: 10 minutes to mark key points, 15 to write questions and 5 to make a summary.

Should I rewrite my notes neatly?

Copying neatly is mostly passive. Converting notes into questions and testing yourself is more useful for memory.

What if my notes are messy or incomplete?

Fill gaps from the lecturer's handout or a textbook, mark unclear parts with a question mark, and ask a coursemate or lecturer about them.

How many questions should I write per topic?

Aim for 8 to 15 questions for a normal lecture. Cover definitions, steps, comparisons and one or two application questions.
