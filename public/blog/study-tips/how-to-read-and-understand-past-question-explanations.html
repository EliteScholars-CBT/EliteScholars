How to Learn From a Wrong Answer in Five Minutes

A wrong answer is the most valuable thing in your study session, but only if you do something with it.

Michael Joseph
·📅 8 November 2026·⏱ 6 min read

Every student does past questions. Far fewer students learn from them. They mark a set, see 14 out of 20, feel a bit sad or a bit proud, and move to the next set. The wrong answers, which show exactly where marks are leaking, get a glance at best. This article gives you a five-minute routine that changes that.

Why seeing the right option teaches so little

When you read "the answer is C" and say "ah, I see", your brain tricks you. The answer feels obvious now that you have seen it, so you assume you would get it next time. Then the same kind of question appears and you pick the same wrong option, because nothing in your thinking has changed.

The real lesson sits in a different question: why did I choose what I chose? Your wrong answer was not random. It came from a belief, a shortcut or a misreading. Find that, and you fix a whole family of future questions, not just one.

The five-minute routine

  1. Minute 1Read the full explanation, not just the answer letter. Say why each wrong option fails
  2. Minute 2Name the cause of your mistake using the categories below
  3. Minute 3Close the book and explain the idea aloud in your own words
  4. Minute 4Write one error log entry: topic, cause, and the rule that fixes it
  5. Minute 5Do two similar questions right away without looking at your notes

The last minute matters most. A fix that you do not test is only a hope. Two quick similar questions show whether the idea has really moved from "I read it" to "I can use it".

Find the cause: five kinds of wrong answer

Different causes need different cures. Label each mistake honestly.

CauseExampleCure
Knowledge gapYou never learned the rule or factRelearn it from notes, then test yourself
MisunderstandingYou learned it, but wronglyRewrite the idea in your own words and check it against your textbook
MisreadingYou missed "not", "except" or "best"Underline key words before looking at options
Careless slipSign error, copied a number wronglyAdd a checking step to your method
Time pressure or guessYou rushed or picked randomlyPractise with a timer and mark your guesses
🔑
Mark your guesses

Put a small star beside any answer you guessed while doing a set. A correct guess is still a gap. Review starred questions as if you had missed them.

Build your error log

An error log is a small notebook with one purpose: to collect your mistakes in one place. Use five columns, or just write one line per mistake.

TopicWhat I chose and whyCauseThe rule that fixes it
Chemistry: molesDivided mass by molar mass upside downMisunderstandingmoles = mass / molar mass
English: concordChose "are" after "news"Knowledge gapNews is singular
Maths: signsLost a minus sign when expandingCareless slipMultiply the sign into every term

Keep entries short. If an entry takes more than a minute to write, you are copying the textbook instead of capturing your own mistake.

A worked example

QuestionA candidate answers a Mathematics question and writes (x - 3)² = x² - 9. How should the error be logged?
  1. Find the cause: the candidate squared each term separately
  2. Name it: misunderstanding of how brackets expand
  3. Fix the rule: (x - 3)² means (x - 3)(x - 3)
  4. Expand carefully: x² - 3x - 3x + 9
  5. Test with a number: x = 5 gives 4 on the left, and 25 - 9 = 16 on the right, so the old answer fails
AnswerCorrect expansion: x² - 6x + 9. Log it as "squaring brackets: expand as a product, never term by term". Then do two more questions with squared brackets.

The number test in that example is a handy trick. Substituting a small value quickly shows whether your rule is wrong.

Review weekly, then hunt for patterns

Once a week, maybe on Saturday morning, read your whole log. After three or four weeks, patterns appear. You may find that half your errors are misreadings, or that one chapter keeps showing up. Those patterns tell you where to spend next week. Fixing your three or four most repeated errors is usually worth more than starting a brand new topic.

Also re-attempt old wrong questions after a week or two without looking at the answer. If you now get them right, the lesson has stuck. If not, go back to the routine.

Common mistake

  • Checking only the letter of the right option
  • Copying the whole explanation into your notes
  • Reviewing mistakes once and never again
  • Feeling bad about a low score

Better way

  • Ask why your option was wrong
  • Write one line in your own words
  • Re-test the same idea after a week
  • Treat the score as a map of where to work

What to do today

  • Pick up a small notebook and rule it into an error log
  • Do 10 past questions and star every guess
  • Run the five-minute routine on each wrong or starred answer
  • Do two similar questions for your biggest mistake
  • Put a weekly log review in your timetable

Frequently asked questions

Why do I keep repeating the same mistakes?

Usually because you check the correct option, nod, and move on. Without finding the reason behind the error, the same thinking leads you to the same wrong choice next time.

What should go in an error log?

The date, the topic, what you chose, the correct answer, the cause of the mistake and the rule or fact that fixes it. One or two lines per entry is enough.

How many wrong answers should I review?

All of them in a session, but spend the full five minutes only on the ones that reveal a real gap. A silly slip needs a note, not a lecture.

Should I also review questions I guessed correctly?

Yes. A lucky guess hides a gap. Mark guesses as you go and treat them like wrong answers during review.

Is an error log better on paper or on a phone?

Use whichever you will actually open every week. A small notebook works well because you can flip through it before a paper.
