Active Recall vs Re-reading: Why Highlighting Is Fooling You

Re-reading and highlighting feel productive but leave you with a false sense of knowing. Testing yourself, and spacing those tests, is what actually builds exam-day memory.

Why Re-reading Feels Like Learning

You read Chapter 4 on Monday. On Wednesday you read it again, and it felt easy. You even recognised the sentences. So you tell yourself, "I know this one." Then the test paper comes, the question is phrased differently, and your mind goes blank.

What happened? Re-reading builds recognition, the feeling that something looks familiar. Exams demand recall, the ability to produce the answer with nothing in front of you. These are two different skills, and practising the first does not automatically give you the second.

The trap is that re-reading is smooth and comfortable. Easy study feels productive. Hard study, where you stare at a blank page trying to remember, feels like failure. In truth it is the opposite: the struggle is the part that makes the memory stick.

Passive study

  • Reading the chapter a second time
  • Highlighting whole paragraphs
  • Copying notes neatly
  • Watching someone solve a problem

Active study

  • Closing the book and writing what you remember
  • Answering past questions cold
  • Making flashcards from your own notes
  • Solving the problem yourself first, then checking

What Active Recall Actually Is

Active recall means forcing your brain to retrieve information before you check whether you are right. Every time you pull a fact out of memory, the brain treats it as important and makes the path to it stronger. Looking at the answer again does not do this.

Examples you can start today:

  • Blank-page recall. Close your notes. Write everything you remember about "photosynthesis" for three minutes. Then open the notes and add what you missed in a different colour.
  • Question cards. Put a question on one side of a card and the answer on the other. Shuffle and test yourself.
  • Past questions. This is active recall in its purest form. Attempt the question before looking at the answer, every time.
  • Teach it out loud. Explain the topic to a friend, a sibling, or the wall. Where you stumble is where your gap is.
🔑
The rule to remember

Never look at the answer until you have committed to your own. A guess you got wrong teaches you more than an answer you only read.

A Worked Example: Turning a Paragraph Into Questions

Here is a short paragraph from an SS2 Biology note: "Osmosis is the movement of water molecules from a region of high water potential to a region of low water potential through a partially permeable membrane." Many students would highlight it and move on. Instead, convert it into questions you can test.

QuestionTurn the osmosis definition into three recall questions and answer them without looking
  1. Question 1: What is osmosis? Answer from memory first
  2. Question 2: In which direction does water move, from high or low water potential?
  3. Question 3: What kind of membrane is needed?
  4. Check your answers against the note and mark each right or wrong
AnswerWater moves from high water potential to low water potential across a partially permeable membrane. Any question you missed goes into tomorrow's review pile

Notice that three questions came from one sentence. That is the point. Good notes are raw material, and your job is to turn them into questions.

Spaced Repetition: Testing at the Right Moments

Memory fades. This is normal, not a sign that you are weak. The trick is to test yourself just as you are about to forget, because that is when retrieval does the most good. This is called spaced repetition.

A practical schedule for any topic:

  1. Day 1Learn the topic and make your questions
  2. Day 2First test, cold, from memory
  3. Day 5Second test, only on what you got wrong
  4. Day 12Third test, mixed with other topics
  5. Day 30Final check before it goes into your exam revision

You do not need an app for this. A simple box with cards, or a notebook page with a "Day 2, Day 5, Day 12" tick list, is enough. What matters is that you come back, not how fancy your system is.

How to Use This for WAEC, NECO and JAMB

Start with how the exams themselves work. Past questions repeat ideas across years, so every question you attempt cold is a rehearsal for the real paper.

  1. Pick one topic per session, for example "mole concept" or "demand and supply".
  2. Test first. Do 10 past questions on it before opening your notes.
  3. Mark and sort. Wrong answers go on a "weak list". Right answers still need a quick check that you did not guess.
  4. Study only the gaps. Read your notes for the weak list, not the whole chapter.
  5. Retest the same questions two days later.

For calculation subjects like Maths, Physics and Chemistry, active recall means working from a blank page. Write the formula first, with no peeking. Then solve a problem. Then compare. Watching a solved example feels like understanding, but it only becomes yours when you can reproduce the steps alone.

⚠️
Watch out for the fluency trap

If you finish a topic and feel confident, test yourself anyway. Feeling confident after reading is the least reliable signal you have. A five-question quiz is the honest signal.

Common Mistakes With Active Recall

  • Peeking too early. If you glance at the answer after five seconds, you are back to recognition. Give yourself at least a minute of real effort.
  • Only testing what you already know. It feels nice to get cards right, but the value is in the ones you miss. Spend your time there.
  • Making cards that are too big. "Explain the whole of respiration" is not a card. Break it down: "What is the word equation for aerobic respiration?"
  • Doing it once. One test helps. Spaced tests are what keep it for months.
  • Using it only the week before exams. Start early, even if only 20 minutes a day.

Where Highlighting Still Fits

You do not have to throw your highlighter away. Use it as step one: mark the facts, definitions and formulas that deserve a question. Then close the book and write the question. Highlighting is the shopping list. Active recall is the cooking.

If you want ready-made practice, use past-question quizzes on EliteScholars and attempt each one before checking the explanation. You can also turn your own notes into questions so your revision matches your own syllabus.

What to Do Today

  • Choose one topic you have only read so far
  • Close the book and write everything you remember for three minutes
  • Turn five facts from your notes into questions
  • Answer 10 past questions on the topic before checking any answer
  • Write the questions you missed on a weak list
  • Schedule a retest for two days from now

Frequently asked questions

What is active recall?

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory without looking at your notes, for example by answering a question, writing out a topic from memory or using a flashcard. The effort of retrieving the answer is what strengthens the memory.

Is highlighting a bad way to study?

Highlighting is not harmful, but on its own it is weak. It makes text look familiar without proving you can recall it. Use it only to mark what you will later turn into questions.

How often should I review a topic?

A simple pattern is to test yourself the next day, then three days later, then a week later, then two weeks later. Each time, spend most of your effort on the questions you got wrong.

Can I use active recall for Maths and Physics?

Yes. For calculation subjects, active recall means solving problems from a blank page and writing the formulas from memory, not reading worked solutions again.

How long should one recall session be?

Twenty-five to forty minutes of focused testing on one subject is enough. Short, frequent sessions beat one long night before the exam.
