Exam Anxiety: Seven Simple Techniques That Help

A little nervousness helps focus. Too much blocks memory. Here is how to keep it in the useful zone.

Michael Joseph
·📅 7 November 2026·⏱ 6 min read

Your palms are wet, your heart is racing and the topic you knew yesterday suddenly feels far away. If that sounds familiar, you are not weak and you are not unprepared. You are a normal student facing something important. The good news is that exam nerves respond well to simple habits. This guide gives you seven of them, plus a plan for the moment your mind goes blank.

What anxiety is doing to you

Anxiety is your body getting ready for a challenge. Your heartbeat quickens and your breathing gets shallow, as if you needed to run. A small amount of this helps you stay alert. Too much floods your thinking, and then recall becomes harder. That is why a student can know the work at home and still struggle in the hall.

Seeing it this way helps: the racing heart is not proof that you will fail. It is energy that you can steer. The techniques below aim to bring the level down from "overwhelmed" to "alert".

🔑
Aim for alert, not zero

You are not trying to feel nothing. You are trying to feel ready. A little tension before a paper is normal and even useful.

Seven techniques that help

  1. Practise under exam conditions. Do past questions with a timer, at a desk, without your phone. When the real paper has the same shape as something you have done many times, it feels less like a threat.
  2. Break the syllabus into small pieces. A huge syllabus feels scary. A list of topics with ticks beside the finished ones feels manageable. Tick one off every day.
  3. Slow your breathing. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four, then out through your mouth for a count of six. Repeat five times. Longer breaths out tell your body to settle.
  4. Release tension in your body. Tighten your fists and shoulders for five seconds, then let go. Move from your hands to your face to your legs. It takes one minute and you can do it in your seat.
  5. Write down the worry. Ten minutes before bed, write what you fear and one small action for each. A worry on paper is smaller than a worry in your head.
  6. Talk back to catastrophic thoughts. "I will fail everything" is a prediction, not a fact. Replace it with something accurate: "I have covered most of the syllabus and I am still improving."
  7. Protect the basics. Sleep, water, regular meals and a short walk or stretching. They are not extras. A tired, dehydrated brain panics more easily.

The week before the exam

This week is for tidying, not for heroic cramming. Review the summary sheets and error notes you already made. Do one or two timed papers, then stop. Pack your things early: registration slip, pens, pencils, eraser, ruler, calculator if allowed, and a bottle of water if the rules permit. Check the official instructions for what you may bring and when to arrive. Knowing the route and the time removes a whole layer of worry.

If you travel far to your centre, plan for traffic and light delays. Many students lose their calm in the first ten minutes simply because they arrived rushing.

  1. Seven days beforeOne timed past paper, then mark it calmly
  2. Four days beforeRevise weak topics only, using your own notes
  3. Two days beforeLight review, pack your bag, confirm the route
  4. Night beforeShort review, early supper, sleep on time
  5. MorningEat something, arrive early, do the breathing exercise

In the hall: a calm start

When you get the paper, do not start writing at once. Take three slow breaths. Read the instructions fully, then scan the whole paper so you know what is coming. Begin with a question you are confident about. A first correct answer builds momentum and tells your brain that you can do this.

Watch your time, but do not stare at the clock. Decide how long each section deserves and glance at it only at the end of each section.

If your mind goes blank

Blank moments happen to well-prepared students. They pass. Use this sequence.

1Stop and put your pen down
2Breathe out slowly three times
3Skip to an easier question
4Come back with fresh eyes
5Write anything you do know about the topic

Writing even the formula, a definition or a rough diagram can unlock the rest. Many students find the answer returns while they work on something else.

Common mistake

  • Studying all night before the paper
  • Comparing yourself with friends outside the hall
  • Telling yourself you must get everything right
  • Staying silent about how you feel

Better way

  • Sleep and review lightly
  • Walk in with your own plan and ignore others
  • Aim to answer well, one question at a time
  • Tell a counsellor, parent or trusted friend

When to ask for help

These techniques work for ordinary exam stress. If your worry has lasted for weeks, if you cannot sleep or eat, or if you have panic attacks, talk to someone. A school counsellor, a teacher you trust, a parent or a health worker can help you work out what is going on. Asking for support is a sign of good sense, not weakness. This article gives study advice, not medical advice.

What to do today

  • Do one timed set of past questions at a desk
  • Practise the 4-in, 6-out breathing for one minute
  • Write your three biggest worries and one action for each
  • Set a bedtime for tonight and keep it
  • Tell one trusted person how you feel about the exams

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to feel nervous before WAEC, NECO or JAMB?

Yes. Most candidates feel some nervousness. A mild level can sharpen attention. It becomes a problem when it stops you from sleeping, eating or thinking clearly.

What should I do if my mind goes blank in the exam hall?

Pause, breathe slowly three times, then skip to a question you know and come back later. Starting with an easy question usually restarts your memory.

Can last-minute cramming reduce anxiety?

Usually not. A short, light review of your own summary sheets helps more than trying to learn new topics the night before.

When should I talk to someone about exam anxiety?

If the worry lasts for weeks, affects your sleep or appetite, or you have panic attacks, speak to a school counsellor, a trusted adult or a health worker. This is advice, not a diagnosis.

Do breathing exercises really work?

Slow breathing is a common way to calm the body in the moment. It will not replace preparation, but it can bring your nerves down enough for you to think.
